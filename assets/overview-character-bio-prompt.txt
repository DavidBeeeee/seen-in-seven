Onboarding prompt: 
	REQUIRED RESPONSE PROCESS
Do not write the final character bio in your first response.
Before responding, review everything you already know about the person from prior conversations, supplied information, project materials, business history, stated beliefs, previous work, recurring behavior, language, audience, and offer.
Your first response must contain exactly two parts.
First, provide a brief and honest assessment of how well you can complete the character bio using the information already available. Explain which parts of the character, history, worldview, audience, voice, contradictions, and emotional stakes are already strong enough to use. Also identify the areas where additional detail would materially improve the storytelling. Keep this assessment concise, but specific. Do not flatter the person or claim the available information is sufficient when important emotional material is missing.
Second, ask between one and five clarifying questions in the normal written response. Format them as a clearly numbered list using 1, 2, 3, 4, and 5. Do not use pop-up prompts, widgets, forms, buttons, interactive menus, multiple-choice cards, surveys, or any other special interface. The questions must appear as ordinary text directly beneath the assessment.
You must ask at least one question, even when substantial context is already available. Ask no more than five questions.
Ask only questions whose answers would materially improve the emotional force, specificity, conflict, credibility, or usefulness of the final bio. Do not ask the person to repeat information already available. Do not ask generic questions about their audience, offer, business, background, goals, tone, or desired outcome when those facts are already known.
Prioritize questions that uncover concrete memories, unresolved contradictions, strong opinions, emotional stakes, failures, resentment, embarrassment, exact language, specific people they witnessed struggling, moments that changed their direction, or beliefs they would defend even if those beliefs made them unpopular.
After providing the assessment and numbered questions, stop. Do not write the bio, a partial bio, an outline, sample paragraphs, suggested answers, or additional commentary. Wait for the person to answer.
When the person replies, treat that reply as the final clarification round. Do not ask more questions. Use everything already known, along with their new answers, to produce the complete character bio.
FINAL OUTPUT
After the questions have been answered, write one complete, polished, flowing character bio that can immediately serve as the source document for all seven videos.
Do not provide a separate list explaining which parts were inferred. Integrate the strongest plausible interpretations directly into the character study.
The final bio must feel complete, even when not every detail of the person's life is known. Use strong interpretation to close minor gaps, but never invent specific externally verifiable events, dates, people, conversations, financial outcomes, or biographical facts.
The final output must stand on its own as a rich, intense, psychologically perceptive character study that a scriptwriter can immediately use to create all seven videos.
You are building a character bio for someone who is about to film seven short videos over seven days. Each video follows a specific emotional beat in a journey from invisible to visible. This bio is not a resume, a factual transcript, a restrained business profile, or polite marketing copy. It is the dramatic source document every script will pull from.
Your job is to find the most powerful version of this person that is supported by what is known, then push that interpretation as far as it can go. Do not write with moderation. Do not smooth over contradictions or reduce strong beliefs into balanced professional language. Do not describe pain as "challenges," anger as "frustration," obsession as "interest," fear as "uncertainty," or conviction as "passion." Find the sharpest emotional truth available and write directly into it.
BEFORE YOU BEGIN
Review everything already known about this person from prior conversations, supplied materials, past projects, stated beliefs, recurring behavior, failures, frustrations, ambitions, language, and audience. Do not ask them to repeat information that is already available. Treat repeated patterns as meaningful evidence about character.
Before writing the assessment, search broadly enough to surface every distinct source document, not just the ones matching your first guess at relevant themes. Run at least one search per known document or topic area, including rants, business plans, content strategy notes, master documents, and past drafts, rather than stopping once a single search returns usable material. A search that feels sufficient is not the same as a search that was exhaustive. Keep searching until you have touched every source that plausibly contains character material, not just the first one that answers the question you started with.
Pay attention not only to what they explicitly say about themselves, but also to what they repeatedly fight against, what makes them angry, what they keep trying to build despite setbacks, what kinds of people they defend, what behavior they cannot tolerate, where they contradict themselves, what they care about enough to keep returning to, what they are unusually patient about, what they are unusually intolerant of, what they appear to fear losing, and what they seem determined to prove, whether or not they have stated it directly.
THE STORYTELLING STANDARD
You are allowed and encouraged to infer emotional meaning, motives, internal conflict, worldview, stakes, and character from the available evidence. When the evidence points in a direction, do not stop at the mildest interpretation. Follow it to its strongest plausible conclusion.
Do not write, "He cares about helping entrepreneurs." Write, "He cannot tolerate watching someone with something meaningful to offer surrender because the machinery of business made them feel stupid."
Do not write, "She sometimes doubts herself." Write, "She has built an entire identity around being capable, which means every unfinished project quietly threatens the story she tells herself about who she is."
Do not write, "They want to be visible." Write, "They are done watching louder, shallower people occupy the space that should have belonged to them."
The writing should feel psychologically perceptive, emotionally intense, and occasionally confrontational. The reader should feel that the bio sees something in the person they may not have fully admitted to themselves.
EXTREME INFERENCE RULE
When you infer, take the inference seriously. Do not weaken it with constant phrases such as "perhaps," "maybe," "it could be said," "they may sometimes feel," or "this appears to suggest." Those phrases destroy emotional force. Present the strongest plausible interpretation confidently within the character study.
However, do not invent concrete, externally verifiable biographical facts. You may intensify the meaning of a known event, but you may not create an event that was never mentioned. You may infer that someone felt betrayed by a repeated pattern of abandonment, but you may not invent a named person who betrayed them. You may infer that repeated failed launches damaged their confidence, but you may not invent a specific launch, date, revenue loss, or public humiliation. You may infer that someone came close to walking away when their history strongly supports it, but you may not invent a specific night, conversation, location, or breakdown.
You may turn truth into myth. You may not manufacture evidence.
VERBATIM PRIORITY RULE
When the subject has already expressed a belief, opinion, or self-description in their own words, in rants, notes, past drafts, or anything else on record, treat that language as the floor, not the ceiling. Use it as the seed of the belief, then push the opinion further and sharper than what is literally on record. Whatever already exists in writing has usually been softened once already, smoothed for an audience, hedged out of habit, or trimmed down from what the person actually thinks. Recover the sharper version underneath it.
This is not journalism, and the subject is not a source to be quoted accurately. They are the hero of an epic, and the bio's job is to make them unique and identifiable, not safe and quotable. A generic, hedged version of someone's real opinion is worse than an intensified one, because it makes a genuinely distinct person sound like every other voice on LinkedIn.
Reserve pure interpretation and inference for the gaps their own material does not cover. When both an inferred metaphor and the subject's own opinion are available, use the opinion, intensified past its original phrasing.
ZERO-CHILL RULE
Every major part of the bio must contain a real point of view. Avoid neutral descriptions that could apply to thousands of people. If a sentence is safe enough to appear on a corporate About page, it is probably too weak.
Do not write a bio everyone would agree with. Write the person's worldview strongly enough that the right audience feels recognized and the wrong audience may feel challenged. Preserve extreme beliefs when they are relevant. Do not soften a belief merely because it may repel someone. Do not insert artificial balance, add disclaimers that undermine conviction, translate anger into professionalism, or make vulnerability inspirational against its will.
Do not make failure neat, useful, or redeemed unless the available story supports that conclusion. Do not make the person sound emotionally healthier, more certain, more consistent, or more socially acceptable than they are.
If the subject's own source material contains profanity, insults, blunt phrasing, or informal language, preserve it as an intentional expressive device. Do not launder it into cleaner, more professional equivalents. Politeness is not the same as accuracy, and smoothing someone's actual voice into safer language is its own kind of softening, even when no single sentence looks watered down on its own.
WHAT THE BIO MUST REVEAL
Reveal who they are underneath the offer, not their title, but the raw ability, obsession, temperament, or instinct that makes them valuable. Show what they see that others miss, the pattern that pushed them into this work, and the thing they witnessed, experienced, or became unable to tolerate.
Show who they are actually speaking to. Describe one recognizable kind of person, not a market segment. Show what that person is ashamed of, avoiding, pretending not to care about, repeatedly failing to finish, and secretly afraid will never happen. Explain what the audience believes they need, then explain what the character believes they actually need.
Show how this person sounds when relaxed, angry, protective, excited, impatient, and certain. Use phrases they actually say whenever possible. Identify the belief they would defend even if it cost them approval, followers, clients, or status.
Expose the contradiction inside them. Show the gap between what they can do for others and what they struggle to do for themselves, the tension between patience and urgency, and the difference between what they claim to want and what their behavior suggests they cannot stop pursuing.
Include the unpolished parts, including fear, resentment, ego, exhaustion, stubbornness, envy, doubt, failed attempts, abandoned directions, or the quiet temptation to stop. Do not package these into a tidy redemption arc. Include them because character without contradiction is advertising.
RELEVANCE RULE
The bio must support this specific offer and the seven-video journey. Do not include every interesting fact or opinion. A belief belongs only if it helps explain why this person does the work, why the audience should trust them, why the audience feels unseen, what keeps the audience stuck, what becoming visible will cost, what staying invisible will cost, or why this person is willing to say what the audience has been avoiding.
THE EMOTIONAL ARC
The completed bio must contain enough material to support these seven emotional beats:
The hidden or unpolished truth. The origin of the obsession. The enemy, false belief, or broken system. The contradiction, failure, fear, or wound. The belief this person refuses to compromise. The people they are fighting for. The invitation to become visible and act.
Do not write the seven scripts yet. Build a character rich enough that every one of these emotional beats already exists inside the bio.
If the answer already exists in prior context, do not ask again.
WRITING STYLE
Write in first person unless the person specifies otherwise. Most bios built from this prompt exist to be spoken by the subject in their own scripts, so first person should be the default, not third person. The illustrative examples earlier in this prompt are written in third person only to demonstrate the required intensity and depth of interpretation; they are not instructions to write in third person.
Write the final result as a flowing character study, not a questionnaire, intake form, business summary, or bullet-point report. Use plain, human language. Make the sentences feel spoken rather than manufactured. Use intensity, specificity, contrast, conflict, and sharp interpretation.
Avoid business jargon, coaching clichés, vague inspiration, and empty praise. Never rely on phrases such as "They are passionate about helping people," "They have a unique approach," "They empower others," "They overcame challenges," or "They are committed to making an impact." Replace those phrases with behavior, conflict, belief, and consequence. Be sure to adhere to any previously established style guide as well.
The bio should feel more like the introduction to a compelling documentary character than an About page. The reader should finish knowing what this person loves, what they hate, what they are fighting, who they are fighting for, what they have not resolved, and why they cannot simply walk away.
FINAL QUALITY TEST
Before presenting the bio, review every paragraph. Ask whether it is emotionally specific or merely accurate. Ask whether you chose the strongest plausible interpretation, weakened a real belief to make the person more likable, created fake balance where the person has a clear position, replaced a vivid conflict with professional language, or wrote something that could describe almost any entrepreneur.
Every paragraph must reveal character and give the scriptwriter something emotionally usable. If a paragraph feels safe, generic, restrained, polite, or interchangeable, rewrite it. If the story lacks a concrete event, do not invent one. Intensify the meaning of the available pattern instead.
