<engagement_ending_rule>

<open_loop_rule>
A Zeigarnik Retention Gap is one concrete piece of unfinished mental business, not vague suspense. [OPEN LOOP] remains the required output label. Use this shape:
1. Begin from the completed MEAT and reserved CONCLUSION, not from the HOOK.
2. Reveal only the setup or contradiction needed to make one unanswered relationship urgent.

1. KNOWLEDGE TEST: The speaker could say every word immediately before the conclusion event occurred.
2. PREDICTION TEST: The viewer cannot predict the person, event, action, delivery method, evidence, quotation, or result that will resolve the gap.
3. RETENTION TEST: A cold viewer can name the unresolved relationship and wants the answer.

An abrupt pivot from HOOK to OPEN LOOP is allowed. Do not add a bridge merely to make the two sections feel connected, and do not explain, repeat, justify, or continue the HOOK. Judge the OPEN LOOP only by whether it creates a specific unanswered question that the viewer wants resolved.

Do not use generic lines like "and everything changed" or "what happened next surprised me" unless the specific unresolved tension is clear. The open loop must point toward the conclusion without naming the final turn too early.

Never let the open loop become a mini-summary. Do not stack all of these in the open loop: old belief + consequence + reversal + proof + lesson. Save those for MEAT and CONCLUSION.

Read the final open-loop sentence by itself. It must name the unresolved subject and the missing relationship clearly enough that a viewer can feel one specific question. The OPEN LOOP may not announce cognition or the final turn with language such as "I realized," "I learned," "I discovered," "I understood," "it showed me," "it proved," "the truth is," or "the point is." <hook_rule>
The hook is not the beginning of the explanation. It is the pre-story interruption that earns enough involuntary attention for the open loop to work.

A strong hook creates a jolt through expectation violation: a charged concrete detail, an odd action, a socially risky admission, an uncomfortable contradiction, a provocative assertion, a playful absurdity, a startling comparison, a direct accusation, or a collision between ideas that normally do not belong together.

For every video, including Videos 3 and 6, build the hook as a pure attention device rather than a miniature argument or chronological story beat. It may draw from the speaker's wider context or use defensible rhetorical compression, exaggeration, opinion, or performance. - sounds like scene setup, chronology, background, biography, a recap, update, journal entry, progress report, section heading, thesis statement, or summary of the accepted belief

- gives away the conclusion, epiphany, lesson, or full meaning before the open loop

Choose the candidate with the strongest defensible pattern break for a cold viewer. Favor the candidate with the greatest semantic collision or strongest provocative performance. A person merely hovering over delete, sitting in a car, pressing record, feeling nervous, or noticing progress is setup unless another element makes it genuinely strange, charged, or socially risky. The hook must be surprising before the viewer hears the open loop, not merely specific after context is supplied. Keep it short enough to land before the viewer has time to decide whether they care.
